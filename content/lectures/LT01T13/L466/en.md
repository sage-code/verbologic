## The Sound of the Letter I

The letter **I** in French typically represents the pure, close front unrounded vowel `/i/`, as in **île** or **lit**. Unlike the English 'ee' sound in *meet*, which often glides slightly, the French `/i/` is held exceptionally tense, steady, and short. When placed before another vowel, it frequently turns into the semi-vowel glide `/j/`, similar to the 'y' in English *yes*.

## How to Produce the Sound

Spread your lips firmly to the sides in an exaggerated, wide smile without rounding them. Arch the front body of your tongue high up toward the hard palate, keeping the tip resting firmly behind your lower front teeth. Keep the vocal cords vibrating smoothly, and release the sound cleanly without diphthongizing it into an 'ee-uh' glide.

## Spelling and Accents

The standard letter **i** is used across all word positions, from beginnings like **ici** to word ends like **ami**. It often carries diacritics: a circumflex (**î**) historically marking a dropped consonant as in **île**, or a tréma (**ï**) indicating that it must be pronounced distinctly rather than fused with an adjacent vowel, as in **maïs**. Additionally, combinations like **in** or **im** before consonants turn into the nasal vowel `/ɛ̃/`.

## Variants and Pitfalls

The main shift occurs when **i** is immediately followed by another vowel, where it shifts from the full vowel `/i/` to the glide `/j/`, as seen in **ciel** `/sjɛl/`. English speakers frequently make the mistake of relaxing this sound to the lax English short 'i' sound `/ɪ/` found in *sit*. Be sure to maintain absolute muscular tension so words like **il** or **ville** do not sound slack or dull.
