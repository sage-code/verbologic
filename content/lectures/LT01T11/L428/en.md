## The Sound of W

The letter **W** is not part of the traditional Italian alphabet and appears only in loanwords, acronyms, or proper names. Depending on the origin of the word, it represents either the voiced labiodental fricative `/v/` (as in German borrowings) or the voiced labio-velar approximant `/w/` (as in English terms). English speakers will easily recognise both sounds, though Italian speakers often assimilate English `/w/` slightly toward a softer vowel-like glide.

## How to Say It

For words of German origin like *wurstel*, touch your upper front teeth lightly to your lower lip and vibrate your vocal cords to make the `/v/` sound. For modern English borrowings like *web* or *weekend*, round your lips tightly as if saying 'oo' and raise the back of your tongue toward the soft palate to produce `/w/`. Transition smoothly into the following vowel without pausing.

## Spelling and Usage

You will only encounter **W** in foreign borrowings, brand names, and modern international terminology. In colloquial Italian handwriting and texting, a solitary capital **W** is also widely used as a shorthand symbol for *viva* ('long live!'), while an inverted **W** (looking like an **M**) stands for *abbasso* ('down with!'). The letter name itself is **doppia vu** or occasionally **doppia vi**.

## Comparing Pronunciations

Be mindful of the word's linguistic origin to choose the right sound: use `/v/` for German-derived terms like *wurstel* and `/w/` for English imports like *webcam*. A common pitfall for learners is pronouncing every **W** as an English `/w/`, which sounds incorrect in established terms like *water* (toilet) pronounced `/ˈva.ter/`. Listen closely to native speakers, as some older borrowings fully retain the `/v/` pronunciation.
