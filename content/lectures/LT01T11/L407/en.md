## The Sounds of C

In Italian, the letter **C** represents two entirely different sounds depending on the vowel that follows it. Before **a**, **o**, **u**, or consonants, it is a hard `[k]` sound just like the English *k* in *cat*, but completely unaspirated without a puff of air. Before **e** or **i**, it becomes a soft `[tʃ]` affricate, matching the clear *ch* in English *church*.

## How to Pronounce C

For the hard sound `[k]`, press the back of your tongue firmly against your soft palate, build up air pressure, and release it crisply without breathing out heavily. For the soft sound `[tʃ]`, place the tip and blade of your tongue against the roof of your mouth just behind your upper teeth. Release the tongue tip slightly into a brief *sh* fricative to create the classic Italian ch-sound.

## Spelling Rules and Silent Letters

Italian spelling uses silent letters systematically to preserve these two sounds across all vowel combinations. To keep the hard `[k]` sound before **e** or **i**, Italian inserts an **h**, as in **amiche** (`/aˈmi.ke/`). Conversely, to produce the soft `[tʃ]` sound before **a**, **o**, or **u**, a silent **i** is added, as seen in **cioccolato** (`/tʃok.koˈla.to/`).

## Comparing Contexts and Common Pitfalls

Notice the sharp contrast between hard words like **casa** (`/ˈka.za/`) and **conto** (`/ˈkon.to/`) versus soft words like **cena** (`/ˈtʃe.na/`) and **cinema** (`/ˈtʃi.ne.ma/`). English speakers often make the mistake of aspirating the hard `[k]` with an exaggerated puff of breath or inadvertently pronouncing the silent **h** as a consonant. Another common error is fully pronouncing the silent **i** in combinations like **ciao**, which should be purely two syllables (`/ˈtʃa.o/`).
