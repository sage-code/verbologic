## The Sound of N

In Italian, **N** is primarily an alveolar nasal `/n/`, very similar to the English 'n' in *no*. However, it naturally assimilates to following consonants, becoming a velar nasal `[ŋ]` before sounds like `[k]` and `[ɡ]` (as in *banco*), or a labiodental nasal `[ɱ]` before `[f]` and `[v]` (as in *invidia*). Unlike English, double **nn** is held for noticeably longer and must be distinctly lengthened.

## How to Produce the Sound

Place the tip or blade of your tongue against the alveolar ridge just behind your upper teeth to block airflow through the mouth. Lower your soft palate so air resonates freely through your nasal cavity while your vocal cords vibrate. For variations before `[k]` or `[ɡ]`, the back of your tongue raises instead, while before `[f]` or `[v]`, your upper teeth rest on your lower lip.

## Spelling and Combinations

The letter **N** can appear at the beginning, middle, or end of words, as well as doubled (**nn**) between vowels. It regularly combines with **g** in the digraph **gn**, which represents the palatal nasal sound `/ɲ/` (as in *gnocchi*), where the 'n' loses its independent identity entirely. When written double as in *notte* or *anno*, make sure to prolong the nasal sound to avoid changing the word's meaning.

## Variants and Common Pitfalls

Listen to the difference between standard `/n/` in *notte*, the back-of-the-throat `[ŋ]` in *ancora*, and the lip-teeth `[ɱ]` in *inverno*. A common mistake for English speakers is reducing or dropping nasal consonants, or failing to distinguish single **n** from double **nn** (such as confusing *ano* and *anno*). Avoid turning **gn** into a separate `[ɡ]` plus `[n]`; it is always a unified palatal glide.
