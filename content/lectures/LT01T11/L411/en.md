## The Sound of the Letter G

The Italian letter **G** has two main sounds depending on the vowel that follows it. Before **a**, **o**, **u**, or consonants, it is a 'hard' voiced velar stop `/ɡ/`, identical to the 'g' in English *go*. Before the front vowels **e** and **i**, it becomes a 'soft' affricate `/dʒ/`, exactly like the 'j' in English *jam*.

## How to Pronounce It

For the hard sound `/ɡ/`, press the back of your tongue against your soft palate, build up air pressure, and release it while vibrating your vocal cords. For the soft sound `/dʒ/`, place the tip and blade of your tongue behind your upper teeth against the alveolar ridge, then release the air with friction. Remember that Italian vowels following both sounds must remain crisp and un-aspirated.

## Spelling Rules and Contexts

To keep the hard `/ɡ/` sound before **e** and **i**, Italian inserts a silent **h**, as seen in *spaghetti* or *ghiaccio*. Conversely, to produce the soft `/dʒ/` sound before **a**, **o**, or **u**, a silent **i** is added, as in *giallo* or *gioco*. Double **gg** lengthens the sound, creating a punchy stop in *leggere* or *aggettivo*.

## Comparing Sounds and Common Mistakes

English speakers often mistakenly pronounce the silent **i** in soft combinations, turning *giorno* into three syllables instead of two. Another frequent trap is reading **gh** as an English 'gh' or 'j', whereas in Italian it is always a hard `/ɡ/` as in *ghetto*. Compare *gatto* (`/ˈɡat.to/`) with *gelato* (`/dʒeˈla.to/`) to hear the fundamental contrast clearly.
