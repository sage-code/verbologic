## The Sound of O

The letter **O** in European Portuguese represents several distinct vowels, but when stressed and open, it produces `[ɔ]`, exactly like the vowel in the British English word "thought" or "cloth". Unlike Brazilian Portuguese or English, unstressed **O** in European Portuguese nearly always reduces to an unstressed `[u]` sound (like in "put"). It can also represent a close `[o]` or a nasal `[õ]` depending on accent marks and neighbouring consonants.

## How to Pronounce It

To produce the open sound `[ɔ]`, drop your lower jaw moderately low while keeping your lips loosely rounded into an oval shape. Position your tongue low in the back of your mouth, ensuring the throat stays open and relaxed. Do not let the sound glide into an "ow" or "oh-oo" diphthong; keep the vowel pure and steady throughout.

## Spelling Rules

When stressed, **O** can be written with an acute accent (**ó**) to explicitly signal the open sound `[ɔ]`, as in **avó** (grandmother). A circumflex (**ô**) marks the closed quality `[o]`, as in **avô** (grandfather). Without accents, spelling alone does not always reveal whether a stressed **O** is open or closed, so vocabulary must be learned individually. At the end of unstressed words or before non-nasal consonants without stress, it is consistently pronounced as `[u]`.

## Comparing Variations and Common Pitfalls

The most common mistake English speakers make is pronouncing unstressed final **O** as a rounded diphthong `/oʊ/` instead of a short, muted `[u]`. For instance, in **olho** (`/ˈɔ.ʎu/`), the first vowel is open `[ɔ]`, but the final vowel reduces completely to `[u]`. Another crucial distinction is between open `[ɔ]` in **avó** and closed `[o]` in **avô**, where confusing the vowel changes the meaning from "grandmother" to "grandfather".
