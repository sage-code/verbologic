## The Sound of D

The letter **D** primarily represents the voiced alveolar stop `/d/`. Unlike in many European languages where it touches the back of the front teeth, in English the tongue contacts the alveolar ridge behind the teeth. In unstressed syllable endings or past-tense endings like '-ed', its pronunciation can adjust to `/t/` or `/ɪd/`.

## How to Pronounce It

Place the tip of your tongue firmly against the **alveolar ridge**, the bumpy gum area right behind your upper teeth. Build up air pressure behind this blockage, then release it crisply while vibrating your vocal cords. Ensure the tongue does not slide down onto the teeth themselves.

## Spelling and Positions

**D** appears at the beginning, middle, and end of words, such as in *door*, *ladder*, and *wind*. Double **dd** typically indicates that the preceding vowel is short, as seen in *middle* and *pudding*. Notably, the letter **d** is silent in several common words, including *Wednesday*, *handsome*, and *sandwich*.

## Variants and Pitfalls

Be careful with the regular past tense suffix **-ed**: it is pronounced `/d/` after voiced sounds (*cleaned*), `/t/` after voiceless sounds (*hoped*), and `/ɪd/` only after roots ending in 't' or 'd' (*wanted*). Non-native speakers often produce an overly dental `/d̪/` instead of an alveolar `/d/`, or pronounce the 'e' in *-ed* when it should remain silent.
