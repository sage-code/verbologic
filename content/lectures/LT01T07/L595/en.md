## The Sound of T as /tʃ/

In certain environments, the letter **T** is palatalised into the voiceless postalveolar affricate `/tʃ/`, sounding just like the **ch** in *church*. This typically happens before unstressed high vowels or the suffix *-ure*. It differs noticeably from the standard crisp `/t/` stop heard at the start of words like *tea*.

## How to Pronounce /tʃ/

Begin by placing the tip and blade of your tongue against the alveolar ridge behind your upper front teeth to block the airflow completely. Then, release the seal slightly so the air pushes through with friction, creating a sharp hiss while rounding your lips slightly. Keep your vocal cords still, as this sound is entirely unvoiced.

## Spelling Patterns

This affricate value of **T** most commonly appears before vowels in suffixes such as *-ure* (as in *nature* and *future*) or *-ual* (as in *actual*). In formal writing, the spelling remains an ordinary letter **T**, never transformed into **CH**. Pay attention to whether the syllable following **T** is unstressed, as this triggers the sound change.

## Contrasting Sounds

Compare the `/tʃ/` in *nature* (`/ˈneɪ.tʃər/`) with the regular stop `/t/` in *native* (`/ˈneɪ.tɪv/`) or the soft `/ʃ/` in *nation* (`/ˈneɪ.ʃən/`). A frequent mistake for non-native speakers is pronouncing *nature* with a hard `/t/` followed by a pure `/uː/` (sounding like *nay-tyoor*). Aim for a smooth, natural English *ch* sound instead.
