## The Sound of T

The letter **T** represents the voiceless alveolar plosive `/t/`. Unlike in many European languages where the tongue touches the upper front teeth, the English `/t/` is articulated higher up against the gum ridge behind your teeth. In stressed initial positions, it is produced with a crisp puff of air known as aspiration.

## How to Say It

Press the tip of your tongue firmly against the **alveolar ridge**, which is the hard bump right behind your top front teeth. Build up air pressure behind your tongue without vibrating your vocal cords. Then, release the tongue briskly to let out a clean, energetic burst of air.

## Spelling and Patterns

The letter **T** is one of the most frequent consonants in the English alphabet. It appears in basic words, doubles as **tt** in words like *butter*, and combines with **h** to create the distinct digraph **th** (`/θ/` or `/ð/`). In endings like *-tion*, it regularly shifts into the `/ʃ/` sound as in *action*.

## Accents and Variations

Pay attention to regional differences when pronouncing **T**. In American English, a single **t** between vowels softens into a quick tap `[ɾ]` (sounding almost like a light *d* in *water*), whereas British speakers often replace final or syllable-ending **t** with a glottal stop `[ʔ]` in words like *cat*. Be careful not to press your tongue against your teeth, or your speech will sound flat and non-native.
