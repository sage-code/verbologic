## The Sound of French ‘ch’

In words borrowed relatively recently from French, the digraph **ch** represents the voiceless postalveolar fricative `/ʃ/`. This is identical to the standard English **sh** sound found in *shoe* or *wish*. Unlike the default English **ch** sound `/tʃ/`, there is no initial 't' stop before the friction begins.

## How to Produce /ʃ/

Round your lips slightly and flare them forward into a soft trumpet shape. Raise the blade of your tongue close to the roof of your mouth, just behind the tooth ridge, without making complete contact. Push a steady stream of unvoiced air through the channel to create a soft, continuous hiss.

## Spelling and Patterns

This `/ʃ/` pronunciation regularly marks French loanwords such as *chef*, *chic*, *machine*, and *brochure*. The letter combination **ch** is also common at the start of native words, but in those cases it almost always sounds like `/tʃ/`. Watch out for place names of French or Native American origin (like *Chicago* or *Michigan*), which also use `/ʃ/`.

## Avoiding Common Pitfalls

Learners frequently mispronounce these French borrowings using the default English `/tʃ/`, saying 'tchef' instead of */ʃef/* or 'macheen' with an affricate. Compare the soft `/ʃ/` in *machine* and *cache* to the punchy `/tʃ/` in *match* and *cheese*. When encountering unfamiliar vocabulary, check the word's etymology, as Greek loans yield `/k/` while French ones yield `/ʃ/`.
