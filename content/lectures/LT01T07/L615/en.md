## The Voiced Dental Fricative

The digraph **th** in words like *this* represents the voiced dental fricative, written in IPA as `/ð/`. Unlike its unvoiced twin `/θ/` in *think*, this sound requires your vocal cords to vibrate during production. Many learners mistake it for `/d/` or `/z/`, but airflow must pass continuously between the tongue and teeth without stopping.

## How to Produce /ð/

Place the tip of your tongue lightly against the edges of your upper front teeth, or slightly between your teeth. Gently push air out around the sides while vibrating your vocal cords to make a buzzing sound. Keep your tongue relaxed so that the air flows smoothly instead of bursting out like a `/d/`.

## Spelling Patterns for Digraph TH

The letters **th** spell both `/ð/` and `/θ/` with no visual distinction in standard English spelling. The voiced sound `/ð/` typically appears in core functional words like *the*, *that*, *then*, and *with*, as well as internally between vowels as in *breathe* and *weather*. Capitalisation follows the normal rules, appearing as **Th** at the start of sentences.

## Comparing /ð/ with Other Sounds

Do not replace `/ð/` with a standard `/d/` or `/z/`, turning *this* into *dis* or *zis*. It is also crucial to distinguish it from the voiceless partner `/θ/`, seen in minimal pairs like *thy* (`/ðaɪ/`) versus *thigh* (`/θaɪ/`). Keep the vocal cords buzzing throughout the entire duration of the sound to maintain clarity.
