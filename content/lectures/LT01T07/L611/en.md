## The Sound of -gh(t)

In modern English, the combination **-ght** is pronounced simply as a crisp `/t/`, while the preceding letters **-gh-** are completely silent. Historically representing a rough guttural fricative, this sound disappeared centuries ago, leaving only a lengthened or diphthongal vowel before the final `/t/`. Learner languages with active guttural sounds like German or Russian should take care never to pronounce the **gh**.

## How to Pronounce -gh(t)

Pronounce the preceding vowel clearly and transition immediately into a standard English `/t/`. Place the tip of your tongue against the alveolar ridge just behind your upper teeth, briefly block the airflow, and release it with a light puff of air. Keep your vocal cords completely still, as this sound is unvoiced.

## Spelling and Context

The cluster **-ght** appears primarily at the end of Germanic-origin words, such as *night*, *bright*, and *daughter*. It regularly signals that the preceding vowel letter is pronounced as a diphthong or long vowel (e.g., **i** becomes `/aɪ/` as in *fight*, and **ou** or **au** becomes `/ɔː/` as in *bought* and *caught*). It never occurs at the beginning of words.

## Common Pitfalls

The most common mistake is attempting to pronounce the silent **gh** as a scraping `/x/`, a `/k/`, or a `/g/`. Remember that pairs like *night* and *knight*, or *right* and *rite*, are complete homophones pronounced strictly with `/t/` at the end. Focus on smoothly linking the vowel directly to the final alveolar stop.
