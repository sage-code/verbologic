## The Sound of L

The letter **L** primarily represents an alveolar lateral approximant. In English, it has two main allophones: a **clear L** (`/l/`) before vowels and a velarised **dark L** (`/ɫ/`) at the ends of words or before consonants. The dark variant has a hollow, back-of-the-throat quality that does not exist in standard varieties of many other languages.

## How to Pronounce It

For the clear `[l]`, place the tip of your tongue against the tooth ridge behind your upper front teeth and let air flow around the sides. For the dark `[ɫ]`, keep the tongue tip contact but simultaneously raise the back of your tongue toward the soft palate. Keep your vocal cords vibrating throughout, as both variants are fully voiced.

## Spelling Patterns

The letter appears as a single **l** in words like *late* or doubled as **ll** after short vowels as in *bell* and *yellow*. It can become syllabic at the end of words ending in *-le* such as *table* (`/ˈteɪ.bl̩/`). Note that **L** is completely silent in several common words, including *walk*, *talk*, *half*, *could*, and *salmon*.

## Comparing Variations

Compare the crisp clear sound in *leaf* (`/liːf/`) with the deeper, throatier dark sound in *feel* (`/fiːɫ/`). A frequent mistake for learners is substituting a clear `[l]` in positions where native speakers expect a dark `[ɫ]`, or replacing the dark `[ɫ]` entirely with a `[w]` sound. Focus on keeping the tongue tip touching the alveolar ridge even when producing the deeper velarised tone.
