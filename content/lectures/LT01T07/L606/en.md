## The Sound of Initial 'gn-'

In modern English, the initial digraph **gn-** is pronounced simply as `/n/`. The letter **g** is entirely silent, meaning it sounds identical to a standalone English `n` as in *name*. Learners frequently make the mistake of trying to pronounce both letters, but English has completely dropped the historical hard `g` at the start of words.

## How to Produce the Sound

Place the tip of your tongue firmly against the alveolar ridge, right behind your upper front teeth. Lower your velum (soft palate) so air flows freely through your nose while keeping your vocal cords vibrating. Completely suppress any urge to drop the back of your tongue or make a throat contact before releasing the sound.

## Spelling and Patterns

This spelling cluster is relatively rare and almost always appears at the beginning of words borrowed from Greek, Germanic, or Romance roots, such as *gnat*, *gnaw*, and *gnome*. It only occurs before vowels in word-initial position. Note that inside words or across morpheme boundaries, the **g** can sometimes awaken, as seen in *signal* compared to *sign*.

## Common Pitfalls

The most critical error for non-native speakers is pronouncing the `g` as a voiced velar plosive, saying `/ɡnoʊm/` instead of `/noʊm/`. Compare *gnat* `/næt/` with *net* `/nɛt/` to hear how the consonant onset is identical despite the unusual spelling. Always treat word-initial **gn-** as a simple orthographic mask for the standard nasal sound `/n/`.
