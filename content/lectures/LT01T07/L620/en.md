## Sound of 'ph'

The digraph **ph** almost always represents the voiceless labiodental fricative `/f/`, exactly like the single letter **f**. It appears primarily in loanwords borrowed from Greek, such as *philosophy* and *alphabet*. Unlike some languages that replace it with a simple 'f', English preserves the classical **ph** spelling while pronouncing it identically to `/f/`.

## How to Pronounce /f/

Gently rest your upper front teeth against the inner edge of your lower lip. Push air smoothly out through the narrow gap between your teeth and lip without vibrating your vocal cords. Keep the airflow continuous and steady, just as you would when making an English **f** sound.

## Spelling Patterns

The combination **ph** occurs at the beginning, middle, or end of words derived from Greek roots, such as *phase*, *sphere*, and *graph*. Be careful with compound words like *shepherd* or *uphill*, where **p** and **h** belong to different root syllables and do not form a single `/f/` sound. In modern English, **ph** is rarely substituted with **f**, so memorizing the Greek origin helps with correct spelling.

## Common Traps and Variations

A rare variation is found in the British pronunciation of *nephew*, which some traditional speakers pronounce with `/v/`. Learners often mistakenly attempt to pronounce both the 'p' and the 'h' sequentially, creating an awkward `/p-h/` cluster in words like *phone*. Treat the digraph as a single unit and simply pronounce an ordinary `/f/`.
