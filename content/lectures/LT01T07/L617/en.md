## The Sound of CH

The digraph **ch** most commonly represents the voiceless postalveolar affricate `/tʃ/`, as in *chair* and *rich*. It blends an initial stop with an immediate friction release, sounding identical to the sound in *cheese*. Depending on word origin, it can also sound like `/k/` in Greek borrowings or `/ʃ/` in French borrowings.

## How to Say It

Start by pressing the tip and blade of your tongue firmly against the alveolar ridge just behind your upper teeth to block airflow, as if making a `/t/`. Then, release the seal slightly so the air bursts through with a turbulent friction sound, keeping your lips slightly flared and your vocal cords completely still. The transition from the stop to the fricative must be sharp, unified, and unvoiced.

## Spelling and Patterns

In English spelling, **ch** appears at the beginning, middle, or end of words, such as *church* and *much*. After a short vowel, this same sound is typically written with **tch**, as in *catch* or *match*. Words where **ch** sounds like `/k/` (like *echo*) or `/ʃ/` (like *chef*) usually reflect Greek or French etymology, respectively.

## Comparing CH Sounds

Compare the regular affricate in *chair* `/tʃ/` with the hard `/k/` in *school* and the soft `/ʃ/` in *machine*. Learners often confuse `/tʃ/` with pure `/ʃ/`, saying *share* instead of *chair*, or mistakenly insert voice to make `/dʒ/`. Pay close attention to word origins to avoid pronouncing scientific Greek terms like *chorus* with an affricate.
