## The Sound of 'th'

The digraph **th** most famously represents `/θ/`, the voiceless dental fricative heard in **think**. It has no vibration of the vocal cords and does not exist in standard varieties of languages like German or Romanian. Unlike a simple `t` or `s`, air flows continuously over the tip of the tongue as it rests against the upper teeth.

## How to Produce /θ/

Gently place the tip of your tongue between your upper and lower front teeth, or lightly against the back edge of your upper incisors. Blow air gently outward over the tongue without letting your vocal cords vibrate. Ensure you do not bite down hard or stop the airflow completely, as that would turn the sound into a `t`.

## Spelling and Patterns

In English spelling, the two letters **t** and **h** consistently join to make either `/θ/` or its voiced counterpart `/ð/`. The voiceless sound `/θ/` regularly appears at the start of content words like **thank** or **thought**, and at the end of words like **path** and **breath**. In rare loanwords of Greek or French origin, such as **thyme** or names like **Thomas**, the digraph is exceptionally pronounced as a simple `t`.

## Comparing Sounds and Common Pitfalls

Be careful to distinguish voiceless `/θ/` in **thin** from voiced `/ð/` in **then**. Learners often substitute familiar sounds, turning **think** into **sink** with an `s`, or **tink** with a `t`. Avoid pressing your tongue against your alveolar ridge; keeping the tip strictly dental ensures the characteristic soft friction rather than a hard stop.
