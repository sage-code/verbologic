## The Sound of Soft 'T'

When the letter **T** appears before suffixes like *-ion*, *-ial*, or *-ient*, it undergoes palatalization and makes the unvoiced postalveolar fricative `/ʃ/`. This is identical to the common English **sh** sound heard in words like *ship*. Instead of producing a sharp stop `/t/`, airflow remains continuous, creating a smooth, rushing hush.

## How to Produce /ʃ/

Round your lips slightly and flare them forward just a bit. Raise the blade of your tongue toward the roof of your mouth just behind the alveolar ridge, leaving a small channel in the center. Gently push air out through this narrow constriction without vibrating your vocal cords.

## Spelling Patterns

This sound for **T** almost exclusively occurs in Latin-derived suffixes such as *-tion* (as in *station*, *action*), *-tial* (*partial*), and *-tient* (*patient*). The letter itself remains a standard uppercase **T** or lowercase **t**, but its phonetic identity shifts due to historical assimilation with following vowels. You will never find this soft `/ʃ/` value at the very beginning or end of a root word.

## Contrasting Sounds and Pitfalls

Learners often mistakenly pronounce the literal `/t/` sound in words like *station*, saying `/ˈsteɪ.tjən/` or `/ˈsteɪ.tsjon/`. Remember that the entire cluster *-tion* is almost always pronounced as `/ʃən/`. Be careful not to confuse this with the affricate `/tʃ/` found in words like *nature* or the standard stop `/t/` found in *ten*.
