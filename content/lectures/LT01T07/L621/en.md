## The Sound of NG

The digraph **ng** represents the voiced velar nasal sound `/ŋ/`. Unlike a sequence of `/n/` and `/ɡ/`, it is produced entirely in the back of the mouth as a continuous nasal hum. Learners frequently mistake it for a regular 'n' followed by a hard 'g', but in standard English words like *sing*, no audible `/ɡ/` release should be heard.

## How to Produce /ŋ/

Open your mouth slightly and raise the back of your tongue to touch your soft palate, in the same contact position used for `/k/` or `/ɡ/`. Lower your velum so air flows continuously out through your nose rather than your mouth. Turn on your vocal cords to make a resonant hum, ensuring you do not drop the tongue suddenly to release a 'g' click.

## Spelling and Patterns

This sound is most often spelled with the combination **ng**, as in *song* or *ring*. It also commonly appears when the letter **n** precedes the velar consonants `k` or `g`, such as in *think* `/θɪŋk/` and *bank* `/bæŋk/`. In English, `/ŋ/` never occurs at the beginning of a native word.

## Comparing Pronunciations

Notice the difference between *singer* `/ˈsɪŋ.ər/`, which has a pure `/ŋ/`, and *finger* `/ˈfɪŋ.ɡər/`, which requires an added `/ɡ/`. A common pitfall for non-native speakers is pronouncing an explicit 'g' or 'k' at the end of words like *going* or *king*. Avoid finishing the sound with a hard tap; keep it smooth and nasal all the way to the end.
