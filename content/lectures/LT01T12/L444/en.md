## The Sound of the Letter N

The letter **N** typically represents the voiced alveolar nasal sound `[n]`, very similar to the English 'n' in *net*. However, in Spanish, **N** is a chameleon sound that naturally assimilates to match the place of articulation of the following consonant. This means it can sound like `[m]` before bilabials or `[ŋ]` before velars without native speakers even thinking about it.

## How to Pronounce It

For the standard `[n]`, press the tip of your tongue against the alveolar ridge just behind your upper teeth. Keep your vocal cords vibrating while allowing air to stream freely out through your nose. Do not drop the nasal airflow early, and let the tongue naturally shift forward or backward depending on the following consonant.

## Spelling Rules and Patterns

The letter is written as lowercase **n** and uppercase **N**, representing an extremely frequent consonant in Spanish. It can appear at the start, middle, or end of words like *nada*, *vino*, or *pan*. Be careful not to confuse plain **N** with **Ñ**, which is an entirely distinct letter with its own separate entry in the Spanish alphabet.

## Variants and Common Pitfalls

Notice how **N** changes subtly: in *noche* `[ˈno.t͡ʃe]` it is purely alveolar, in *canto* `[ˈkan̪.to]` the tongue touches the upper teeth, and in *un beso* `[um ˈbe.so]` it shifts fully to `[m]`. English speakers often fail to make these natural assimilations, sounding overly stiff. Another common error is adding an audible English 'g' sound when **N** comes before a velar consonant; keep the sound flowing smoothly without extra plosion.
