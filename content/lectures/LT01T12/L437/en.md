## The Sounds of G

The letter **G** in Spanish has three distinct sounds depending on its position and the vowel that follows. Before **a**, **o**, or **u**, it represents either a hard `[ɡ]` or a softer approximant `[ɣ]`, whereas before **e** and **i**, it represents a raspy voiceless sound `[x]`. In Castilian Spanish, this raspy `[x]` is much harsher than the English *h*, resembling the *ch* in Scottish *loch*.

## How to Produce It

For the soft `[ɣ]` heard between vowels in words like *amigo*, bring the back of your tongue near the soft palate without touching it, letting voiced air squeeze through smoothly. For the initial `[ɡ]`, firmly press the back of your tongue against the soft palate to briefly block the airflow, just like in *game*. For the throat sound `[x]` before **e** and **i**, press the back of your tongue close to the uvula and exhale with audible, friction-like scraping without using your vocal cords.

## Spelling Rules

To keep the hard sound before **e** or **i**, Spanish inserts a silent **u**, as in *guitarra* or *guerra*. If the **u** must be pronounced between **g** and **e**/**i**, a diaeresis is added, as in *pingüino*. When **g** stands directly before **e** or **i**, it always takes the harsh `[x]` sound, functioning identically to the letter **j**.

## Variants and Pitfalls

Notice the difference between the hard stop in *gato* `[ˈɡa.to]` and the continuous frictionless airflow in *pago* `[ˈpa.ɣo]`. A common mistake for English speakers is pronouncing every non-harsh **g** as a full, hard stop `[ɡ]`, which sounds unnatural between vowels. Another pitfall is pronouncing **ge** and **gi** like the soft English *j* in *gem* instead of using the raspy friction of `[x]`.
