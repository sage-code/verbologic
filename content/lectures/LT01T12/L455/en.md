## The Sound of X

In European Spanish, the letter **X** most commonly sounds like `[ks]`, much like the *x* in English *taxi*. At the beginning of a word or before a consonant in relaxed everyday speech, it frequently reduces to a simple `[s]` sound. Historically preserved words, particularly Mexican place names like *México*, preserve the older pronunciation where it sounds like a harsh `[x]`, equivalent to the Castilian *j*.

## How to Produce It

For the standard `[ks]` cluster, raise the back of your tongue against your soft palate to make a clean, unvoiced `[k]`, then immediately release the air through your teeth to make an unvoiced `[s]`. Keep the vocal cords still, as Spanish does not voice this cluster into `[gz]` like English often does in *exact*. When it sits at the start of a word, simply start straight with the `[s]` sound.

## Spelling and Distribution

The letter **X** is relatively infrequent in native vocabulary, appearing mainly in Latin-derived prefixes like *ex-* or Greek loanwords. Word-initially, it is quite rare and almost exclusively found in scientific or cultural borrowings like *xilófono*. Note that in modern standard orthography, archaic words previously written with *x* for the `[x]` sound are now regularised to **J** (such as *Quijote*), except in protected proper names.

## Contrasts and Common Traps

English speakers often default to voicing **x** as `[gz]` in words like *examen*, producing an unnatural sound; always keep it unvoiced as `[ks]` or simplified `[s]`. Avoid pronouncing word-initial **x** with a `[k]` prefix, so *xilófono* starts smoothly with `[s]`. Finally, remember that Mexican toponyms like *Oaxaca* or *Texas* retain the velar fricative `[x]`, not the English `[ks]`.
