## The Sound of 'oa'

The vowel digraph **oa** typically represents the 'long O' diphthong `/oʊ/` (or `/əʊ/` in British Received Pronunciation). It starts with a mid-back or central vowel and glides smoothly up toward a rounded `/ʊ/`. Unlike pure monophthongal vowels found in many languages, this sound involves noticeable movement of the lips and tongue.

## How to Produce It

Begin with your tongue positioned in the mid-back area of your mouth and your lips relaxed or slightly rounded. Push the back of your tongue upward slightly while rounding your lips into a tighter circle to finish the glide. Keep the transition completely continuous without inserting a pause between the two phases.

## Spelling Patterns

The spelling **oa** almost exclusively appears in the middle of words, positioned between consonants, as in **boat**, **soap**, and **coat**. It rarely appears at the absolute end of native English words, where **ow** or **o_e** is preferred instead. When followed by the letter 'r', as in **board** or **soar**, it merges into the vowel sound `/ɔː/`.

## Comparisons and Common Pitfalls

Learners often make the mistake of pronouncing **oa** as a flat, single vowel like `/o/` or splitting it into two distinct syllables like `/o.a/`. In English, it functions as a single gliding vowel unit that rhymes with words ending in silent 'e' like **rope** and **hope**. Be cautious before 'r', where words like **coarse** shift entirely to the `/ɔː/` vowel.
