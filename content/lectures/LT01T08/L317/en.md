## The Sound of 'EA'

The digraph **ea** most frequently represents the long close front vowel `/iː/`, exactly like the sound in *see*. However, it is also famous for frequently representing the short open-mid front vowel `/e/`, as heard in *head*. Unlike simple single-letter vowels, its exact sound often depends on word history rather than strict phonetic rules.

## How to Pronounce It

For the primary `/iː/` sound, spread your lips wide into a smile and raise the front of your tongue close to the roof of your mouth. Keep the vocal cords vibrating steadily and sustain the vowel length. If pronouncing the `/e/` variant instead, drop your jaw slightly lower and relax the tongue into a mid-front position without smiling as intensely.

## Spelling Patterns

**EA** is one of the most versatile vowel digraphs in English orthography, appearing across native Germanic vocabulary. It commonly occurs in the middle of words like *meat* and *dream*, or at the end of monosyllables such as *tea* and *sea*. When followed by the letter *r*, it typically shifts into a diphthong or triphthong quality as in *clear* or *bear*.

## Contrasting Sounds and Common Traps

Learners often stumble because **ea** does not have a single fixed sound; compare the long `/iː/` in *read* (present tense) with the short `/e/` in *read* (past tense). Do not automatically pronounce every instance as a long 'ee' sound, which leads to mispronouncing essential words like *breakfast*, *heavy*, and *dead*. Pay special attention to the rare diphthong `/eɪ/` in irregular words such as *break* and *steak*.
