## The Sound of OW

The digraph **ow** represents two major diphthongs in English: `/aʊ/` as in *now* and `/oʊ/` as in *snow*. The `/aʊ/` variant starts with an open front-to-central vowel before gliding smoothly toward a rounded back vowel. The `/oʊ/` sound begins mid-back and glides upward into a tighter rounded shape.

## How to Produce It

For `/aʊ/`, open your jaw wide and keep your tongue low and flat, then immediately glide your tongue up and round your lips into a small circle. For `/oʊ/`, begin with a mid-height vowel while loosely rounding your lips, then purse them more tightly as your tongue pulls upward toward the soft palate. Keep both sounds flowing as continuous glides rather than two separated vowels.

## Spelling and Patterns

The combination **ow** frequently appears at the end of words or before the letter **n** and **l** (such as *clown* or *bowl*). There is unfortunately no universal spelling rule to predict whether it makes `/aʊ/` or `/oʊ/`, though final position often favors `/oʊ/` in words with Old English origins (like *blow*, *crow*). Notice that heteronyms like *bow* (a weapon vs. bending forward) and *row* depend entirely on context for their pronunciation.

## Comparing Pronunciations

Contrast words like *cow* `/kaʊ/` with *low* `/loʊ/` to clearly hear the distinction between the two diphthongs. Learners often default to only one sound, mistakenly saying *slow* with `/aʊ/` or *how* with `/oʊ/`. Pay close attention to homographs like *sow* (female pig `/saʊ/` vs. planting seeds `/soʊ/`) to avoid confusing listeners.
