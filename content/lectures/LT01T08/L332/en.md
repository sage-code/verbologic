## The Sound of "ur"

The letter combination **ur** most often produces the central vowel heard in words like *nurse* and *turn*. In General American it is pronounced as an r-colored vowel `[ɜ˞]`, while in British Received Pronunciation it becomes the long monophthong `[ɜː]`. Learners often confuse it with an open `[a]` or round their lips into an `[u]`, but it should remain completely neutral.

## How to Pronounce It

Keep your lips relaxed and slightly parted, neither rounded into an *o* nor spread wide into an *e*. Place your tongue in the middle of your mouth with the body slightly raised, and if speaking with an American accent, curl the tip backward slightly or bunch the back of the tongue toward the palate. Vocalize steadily from the throat without shifting into a pure *u* sound.

## Spelling Patterns

This spelling is extremely common in native Germanic roots and French loanwords, typically occurring in stressed syllables before consonants as in *burn* and *church*. It also appears word-finally in shorter words such as *fur* and *blur*. When followed by a vowel within a single root, it can sometimes trigger a diphthong like `[jʊər]` as in *furious* or *pure*.

## Accent Variations and Common Traps

Listen closely to the distinction between rhotic American `[tɜːrn]` and non-rhotic British `[tɜːn]`, where the final r-coloring is omitted entirely. A frequent error among foreign speakers is pronouncing the written *u* as an exaggerated back vowel like `[uː]` or `[o]`, leading to mispronunciations like *toorn* for *turn*. Remember that **ur**, **er**, and **ir** generally share the exact same sound in English.
