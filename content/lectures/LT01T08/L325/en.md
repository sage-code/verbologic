## The Sound of 'au'

The digraph **au** usually represents the long open-mid back rounded vowel `/ɔː/` in British English, or `/ɑː/` in General American. It is a steady monophthong rather than a gliding diphthong, unlike its spelling might suggest. In everyday speech, it sounds very similar to the vowel in words like *law* or *caught*.

## How to Produce the Sound

Drop your jaw down and pull your tongue slightly back into your mouth toward the rear of your throat. Round your lips gently without puckering them too tightly into a small circle. Keep the airflow continuous and steady, vibrating your vocal cords without letting your tongue shift position.

## Spelling Patterns

This combination appears most often at the beginning or in the middle of words, such as in *August*, *cause*, and *launch*. At the very end of native English words, the spelling **aw** is used instead, making final **au** rare outside French borrowings like *plateau*. It also regularly surfaces in Latin and Greek roots like *audio* or *auto*.

## Comparing Variations

Be careful not to pronounce **au** as the diphthong `/aʊ/` heard in *cow*, which is a frequent error for speakers of phonetic languages. While British English maintains the rounded `/ɔː/` in *pause*, American English often merges this vowel into the unrounded `/ɑː/`, making *caught* sound identical to *cot*. Avoid inserting an extra 'w' sound unless the following consonant naturally requires lip rounding.
