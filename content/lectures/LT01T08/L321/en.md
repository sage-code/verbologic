## The Sounds of 'OO'

The digraph **oo** primarily represents two different sounds: the long tense vowel `/uː/` as in *moon*, and the short lax vowel `/ʊ/` as in *book*. Unlike standard continental vowels, the short `/ʊ/` sound is relaxed and slightly open rather than fully rounded. In rare cases, it can also produce a short cut sound `/ʌ/` as in *blood* or a broad vowel `/ɔː/` before 'r'.

## How to Form the Sounds

For the long `/uː/`, pull your tongue high and back in your mouth while pursing your lips tightly into a small circle. For the short `/ʊ/`, relax both your lips and tongue so your mouth stays slightly open without tight rounding. Be careful not to make the short sound too sharp or tense like a pure continental 'u'.

## Spelling Patterns

The digraph **oo** appears almost exclusively in the middle of native English root words, though occasionally at the end of playful or borrowed terms like *zoo* or *shampoo*. Most commonly it yields `/uː/`, but before the letter 'k' (as in *look*, *cook*, and *took*) it almost always shifts to `/ʊ/`. Note irregular words such as *good*, *foot*, and *wood*, which also take the shorter vowel.

## Contrasting the Sounds

Compare the long sound in *pool* `/puːl/` and *suit* with the relaxed short sound in *pull* `/pʊl/` and *book*. A common mistake for learners is using the same tense, fully rounded 'u' sound for both varieties, turning *look* into *Luke*. Practice contrasting pairs like *fool* and *foot* to master the distinct tongue tension and lip shape.
