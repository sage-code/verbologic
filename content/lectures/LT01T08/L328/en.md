## The Sound of 'AR'

The phonogram **ar** typically represents the open back vowel followed by an 'r' sound, transcribed as `/ɑːr/` in American English or a long `/ɑː/` in British English. Unlike a pure short 'a', this vowel is articulated deep in the throat with an open jaw. Learners should be careful not to pronounce it as a flat front vowel like the 'a' in 'cat'.

## How to Produce the Sound

Drop your jaw wide and keep your tongue low and flat toward the back of your mouth to form the open `/ɑː/`. As you sustain the vowel, curl the tip of your tongue slightly upward or bunch the tongue body backward to create the rhotic American `/r/` coloring without letting the tongue touch the roof of the mouth. Keep your lips neutral and relaxed throughout.

## Spelling and Patterns

This combination appears frequently as a root vowel digraph in words like **car**, **hard**, and **dark**. At the end of unstressed syllables, such as in **dollar** or **regular**, it reduces to a weak rhotic schwa `/ər/` (or `/ə/` in non-rhotic dialects). Additionally, when preceded by a 'w' sound (as in **warm** or **war**), it usually shifts to `/ɔːr/`.

## Dialects and Common Errors

In General American, the 'r' is clearly pronounced as a rhotic glide `/ɑːr/`, whereas in British Received Pronunciation, the 'r' is silent unless followed by a vowel, leaving a long `/ɑː/`. Non-native speakers often mistakenly trill or tap the 'r' against the alveolar ridge or substitute a tense `/a/` sound. Ensure the sound remains open, deep, and smooth without any consonant flutter.
