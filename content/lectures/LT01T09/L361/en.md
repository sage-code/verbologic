## The Sound of T

The German letter **T** represents a voiceless alveolar plosive, transcribed as `/t/`. At the beginning of a stressed syllable or word, it is strongly **aspirated** (`/tʰ/`), releasing a noticeable puff of air just like in English *tea*. In clusters such as **st** or **rt**, or before unstressed endings, this aspiration is absent or much weaker.

## How to Pronounce It

Place the tip of your tongue firmly against the alveolar ridge just behind your top front teeth to block the airflow completely. Build up slight air pressure behind the closure, then release the tongue sharply downward. Let out a crisp puff of air without vibrating your vocal cords.

## Spelling and Patterns

The letter can appear as a single **t**, a doubled **tt** (indicating that the preceding vowel is short, as in *Mutter*), or combined in digraphs like **th** (which is pronounced as a plain `/t/` in native and loanwords like *Thema*). It also pairs with other consonants in trigraphs such as **tsch** for the `/t͡ʃ/` sound. Final **t** never softens or turns into a flap.

## Watch Out for Traps

Learners with Romance language backgrounds often forget to aspirate word-initial **t**, making words like *Tier* sound almost like *Dir*. English speakers must avoid 'flapping' the letter between vowels: in German *bitten*, you must articulate a crisp, distinct dental/alveolar stop rather than the soft tap heard in American English *butter*.
