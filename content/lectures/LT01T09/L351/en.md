## The Sound of J

In standard native German words, the letter **J** is pronounced as a voiced palatal approximant `/j/`, exactly like the *y* in English *yes*. It does not have the harsh English *dzh* sound in native vocabulary. However, in modern borrowings from French or English, it usually retains the source language's pronunciation (`/ʒ/` or `/dʒ/`).

## How to Produce /j/

To make the German `/j/`, raise the middle body of your tongue close to the hard palate, just as if you were about to say the vowel in *see*. Keep your vocal cords vibrating and your lips relaxed and unrounded. Let the air glide smoothly through the narrow constriction without creating friction or popping.

## Spelling and Patterns

The letter **J** appears predominantly at the beginning of words and roots, preceding vowels as in *Jacke* or *Juni*. Capital **J** and lowercase **j** are standard, and it never occurs at the end of native German words. In some loanwords, such as *Jeans* or *Jongleur*, it preserves foreign sound-spelling correspondences.

## Variations and Common Pitfalls

The most critical error for English speakers is pronouncing German native words like *Jahr* or *ja* with an English *j* sound (`/dʒ/`). Always treat a native German **J** as a English *y*. Pay attention to loanwords like *Job* (`/dʒɔp/`) and *Journal* (`/ʒʊʁˈnaːl/`), where speakers switch intentionally to foreign phonemes.
