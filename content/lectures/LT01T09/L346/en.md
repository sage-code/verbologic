## The Sounds of 'E'

The German letter **E** represents several distinct sounds depending on stress and syllable structure. When long, it sounds like a tense, pure `[eː]` similar to the vowel in 'café', while the short version is an open `[ɛ]` like the vowel in 'bed'. In unstressed grammatical endings, it weakens to a neutral schwa `[ə]`, and before an unstressed final 'r' as in '-er', it merges into a subtle open vowel `[ɐ]`.

## How to Pronounce It

For the long `[eː]`, spread your lips wide into a gentle smile, keep your tongue high and tense toward the front of your mouth, and avoid gliding into an English 'ay' diphthong. For short `[ɛ]`, drop your jaw slightly and relax your tongue muscles. When saying the unstressed `[ə]` at word endings, relax your vocal tract completely and produce a very brief, effortless central murmur.

## Spelling and Patterns

A stressed **E** is pronounced long `[eː]` when doubled (**ee**), followed by a silent **h** (**eh**), or in an open syllable. It is short `[ɛ]` when followed by two or more consonants, such as double letters (**Bett**, **hell**). Almost every grammatical suffix ending in single **-e**, **-en**, or **-em** uses the unstressed schwa `[ə]`.

## Contrasting Sounds and Common Mistakes

English speakers often mistakenly turn long `[eː]` into the diphthong `[eɪ]`, turning *Tee* into 'tay-ee'. Another common error is over-pronouncing the final unstressed **-e** as an English 'ee' (making *bitte* sound like 'bit-tee') instead of using the muted schwa `[ə]`. Notice how the vowel quality changes between the tense long sound in **Beet** `[beːt]` and the lax open sound in **Bett** `[bɛt]`.
