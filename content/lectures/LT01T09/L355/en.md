## The Sound of N

The letter **N** in German most commonly represents the voiced alveolar nasal `/n/`, identical to the English 'n' in 'name'. Before velar stops like **k**, it assimilates to the velar nasal `/ŋ/`, sounding like the 'ng' in 'sink'. In everyday spoken German, an unstressed ending like **-en** frequently reduces so that the **N** becomes syllabic `[n̩]`, forming its own syllable without a distinct vowel sound.

## How to Say It

To produce the standard `/n/`, press the tip of your tongue firmly against the alveolar ridge just behind your upper front teeth. Lower your soft palate to let the airstream pass freely out through your nose while vibrating your vocal cords. When pronouncing the reduced ending **-en**, keep your mouth in the position of the previous consonant and simply release the voice through your nose without adding a separate vowel.

## Spelling and Rules

German **N** can appear alone or doubled as **nn**, which serves as a marker that the preceding vowel is short and lax, as seen in **Kanne** or **Sonne**. It also combines with **g** to form the digraph **ng**, representing the single sound `/ŋ/`. Remember that all German nouns starting with **N**, such as **Name** or **Nacht**, must always be capitalized.

## Tips and Common Pitfalls

English speakers often pronounce the final **-en** ending too clearly as `/ən/`, which can sound overly robotic or artificial in natural conversation. Aim instead for a quick syllabic nasal `[n̩]` in words like **haben** (`/ˈhaːbn̩/`). Watch out also for words where **n** sits directly before **k**, ensuring you shift the articulation back to `/ŋ/` rather than pronouncing a crisp `/n/` followed by `/k/`.
