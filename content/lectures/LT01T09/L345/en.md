## The Sound of D

In German, the letter **D** typically represents a voiced alveolar plosive `/d/` at the start of words and syllables, very similar to the English 'd' in *door*. However, when it appears at the end of a syllable or word, a process called terminal devoicing (*Auslautverhärtung*) turns it into a sharp, voiceless `/t/`. Unlike English speakers, Germans pronounce `/d/` with the tongue firmly against the upper teeth or alveolar ridge, producing a crisper sound.

## How to Pronounce D

Place the tip of your tongue against the alveolar ridge directly behind your upper front teeth to completely block the airflow. For the voiced `/d/` at syllable beginnings, gently vibrate your vocal cords as you release the tongue. If the letter stands at the end of a syllable, stop the voicing entirely and release the air with a crisp `/t/` burst.

## Spelling Patterns

The letter **D** is written as uppercase **D** and lowercase **d**, appearing frequently in native German vocabulary and loanwords. Double **dd** occurs occasionally after short vowels, as in *Pudding* or *Widder*, maintaining the `/d/` sound before a following vowel. Remember that word-final spellings with single **d** are phonetically pronounced as `/t/`, yet spelled with **d** due to related inflected forms like *Bäder* from *Bad*.

## Variants and Common Pitfalls

Notice the stark contrast between syllable-initial `/d/` in *Danke* and syllable-final `/t/` in *Hund* `/hʊnt/` or *Bild* `/bɪlt/`. English speakers frequently forget *Auslautverhärtung* and produce a voiced, soft English 'd' at the end of words, which sounds unnatural in German. Always ensure that words ending in **d** receive a clean, voiceless `/t/` unless an inflected vowel suffix follows.
