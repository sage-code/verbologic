## The Sound of O

The German letter **O** represents two distinct vowel sounds depending on syllable structure: a tense, closed long `/oː/` and a lax, open short `/ɔ/`. English speakers should be careful not to glide the long `/oː/` into a diphthong like the 'o' in 'boat'; it remains completely pure and steady from start to finish. The short `/ɔ/` closely mirrors the British English vowel heard in 'lot' or 'cost'.

## How to Pronounce O

For the long `/oː/`, round your lips tightly into a small circle, keep your tongue pulled back, and tense the vocal tract without letting the jaw close further during the sound. For the short `/ɔ/`, drop your jaw slightly lower and relax the lips into a broader, looser oval shape. Ensure that in both cases, your tongue does not glide towards a 'w' sound at the end.

## Spelling and Patterns

A single **O** is typically pronounced long `/oː/` before a single consonant, at the end of an open syllable, or when doubled (**oo**) as in **Boot**. It is also long when followed by a silent lengthening **h**, as in **Kohl** or **Sohn**. Conversely, it represents the short `/ɔ/` when followed by double consonants or consonant clusters, such as in **Sommer** or **Kopf**.

## Comparing Variants and Common Pitfalls

Contrast the long, tense vowel in **Ofen** `/ˈoːfn̩/` with the short, open vowel in **offen** `/ˈɔfn̩/`. The most frequent error for learners is turning long `/oː/` into an English-style diphthong [oʊ]. Practice keeping your jaw, tongue, and lips entirely motionless while sustaining words like **Rose** and **Obst**.
