## The Sound of Q

In German, the letter **Q** almost never appears alone; it is virtually always paired with **u** as the combination **qu**. Unlike the English pronunciation /kw/, standard German pronounces **qu** as `/kv/`, combining a crisp `[k]` immediately followed by a voiced German `[v]` (like an English *v*). In loanwords borrowed from French, such as *Boutique*, the final **que** simply sounds like a plain `/k/`.

## How to Say It

To produce the standard German **qu**, start by pressing the back of your tongue against your soft palate to make a sharp, voiceless `[k]`. As you release that burst of air, immediately place your upper front teeth lightly against your lower lip to voice a smooth, continuous `[v]`. Transition seamlessly without inserting any pause or vowel sound between the `/k/` and the `/v/`.

## Spelling and Rules

Native German vocabulary strictly uses **Q** followed by **u**, forming words like *Quelle* (spring) or *bequem* (comfortable). You will never see a freestanding **q** without **u** in native German roots. When capitalised at the start of a noun or sentence, only the **Q** is uppercase, while the accompanying **u** remains lowercase (*die Quelle*).

## Comparison and Common Mistakes

English speakers frequently make the mistake of pronouncing German **qu** as `/kw/` (like *quick*), saying */ˈkwɛlə/* instead of the correct */ˈkvɛlə/*. Remember to bring your top teeth to your bottom lip for a firm `[v]` rather than rounding your lips into a `[w]`. The only common exceptions are recent English imports like *Quiz*, which many speakers keep as `/kwɪs/`, and French borrowings ending in *-que*, which are simply `/k/`.
