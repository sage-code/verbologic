## The Sound of C

On its own, the letter **C** is almost exclusively found in loanwords in German, so its sound depends directly on the word's origin. Before back vowels or consonants it usually sounds like a regular English `k` as in *cat*, whereas before front vowels like *e* or *i* it commonly takes the German `ts` sound identical to the letter **Z**. In certain modern English or French borrowings, it may also sound like a soft `s` or blend into common digraphs like **ch**.

## How to Pronounce It

For the hard variant `k`, raise the back of your tongue firmly against your soft palate, build up air pressure, and release it crisply. When pronouncing it as `ts`, start with your tongue tip pressed against the back of your upper teeth as if making a `t`, then instantly release it into a sharp hissing `s`. Keep your lips relaxed and neutral for both sounds.

## Spelling Patterns

Purely native Germanic words rarely use a solitary **C**; native roots rely almost entirely on digraphs like **ch** or **ck**. Standalone **C** marks international vocabulary taken from Latin, Greek, French, or English. It capitalizes normally at the beginning of nouns or sentences (like **Café** or **Cent**), but native German spelling reforms have historically replaced many older Latin **c** spellings with **k** or **z**.

## Variants and Common Pitfalls

Notice the contrast between **Computer** (`/kɔmˈpjuːtɐ/`) with a hard `k` and **Cent** (`/tsɛnt/`) with a crisp `ts`. English speakers often make the mistake of pronouncing words like **Celsius** or **Cent** with a soft English `s` instead of the required German `ts`. Always check whether the following vowel is front or back, and default to `ts` for Latinate academic and financial terms.
