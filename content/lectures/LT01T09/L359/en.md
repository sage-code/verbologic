## Sound of the Letter

In standard German, **R** usually produces a voiced uvular fricative `/ʁ/` at the start of syllables, similar to the French 'r', or vocalises into a central vowel `[ɐ]` at the end of syllables. Unlike the English curled 'r' (`/ɹ/`), the tongue tip never turns upward toward the roof of the mouth. English speakers must avoid substituting their native rhotic sound, as it sounds distinctly foreign in German.

## How to Pronounce It

For the consonant variant `/ʁ/`, keep the tip of your tongue resting behind your lower front teeth and raise the back of your tongue toward the uvula. Let the air flow gently through the narrow constriction to create a soft friction, almost like a gargling motion without water. When followed by a consonant or at the end of a word, simply relax your mouth into an open, unstressed schwa-like vowel `[ɐ]`.

## Spelling and Patterns

The letter **R** appears in both lowercase ('r') and uppercase ('R'), commonly starting words such as **Rot** or doubled as in **Wasser**. Double 'rr' indicates that the preceding vowel is short, as in **Herren**. Word-finally and in the frequent unstressed suffix **-er**, it is almost universally vocalised into `[ɐ]` rather than pronounced as a full consonant.

## Variants and Common Pitfalls

Notice the contrast between the consonant in **Rot** (`/ʁoːt/`) and the vocalised ending in **Vater** (`/ˈfaːtɐ/`). Learners frequently make the mistake of using an English postalveolar approximant or over-rolling an Italian-style trill in northern and central standard contexts. Remember that after long vowels and in unstressed endings, letting it soften into an 'a'-like sound is key to sounding authentic.
